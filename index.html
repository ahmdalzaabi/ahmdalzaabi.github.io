About

Underwater robots can record hours of video on a single dive, but they can only send a few bytes at a time back to the surface over acoustic links. My research asks what a robot should say about what it sees: which animals were present, how many, and when, reported reliably enough for ecologists to trust.

I work in the robotics group of Prof. Jorge Dias, co-supervised by Dr. Federico Renda, and collaborate with marine scientists on surveys of UAE coastal waters.

Research

Semantic compression for underwater monitoring

Instead of streaming raw video, the robot detects ecological events onboard and sends compact, structured reports. The work covers species recognition that runs on embedded hardware, an event format sized for acoustic links, and a way to score how faithfully a transmitted record reflects what actually happened on the dive.

Recognising endangered rays

A detector for the Endangered coach whipray (Himantura uarnak) that works from small amounts of data. It finds rays, rejects look-alikes such as flatfish, and names the species only when confident, because the coach whipray and its close relatives are hard to tell apart even for experts.

Learning from few recordings

Fish identification models often look accurate but fail on new footage. I study how the number of distinct recordings, rather than the number of frames, drives whether a model generalises.
